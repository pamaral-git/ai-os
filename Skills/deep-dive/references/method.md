## Splitting a subject into claims

A famous sentence usually bundles several assertions of different strength. Separate them before engaging, then test each one against the evidence.

Technique: write each assertion as a plain declarative. For each, ask whether it is (a) trivially true, (b) true under a definition that needs stating, (c) partly false, or (d) unfalsifiable. The original session's line, "those who hate you don't win unless you hate them", split into two claims: being hated is unavoidable for anyone visible (safe), and the damage only arrives through your own hatred (partly false, since smears and institutions harm without your participation). The honest reading became: the damage you control lives in your response, not all damage.

Then ask what the speaker's situation adds. The same sentence from a person at the moment of defeat, from a person who has behaved the opposite way, or from a person with an audience, reads differently each time.

## The mechanism chain

The integrated thesis usually takes the form of a sequence in which one state produces the next, with an exit at each stage. The original chain was: wound, story, culprit, list, instrument, closed room. Each stage had an exit supplied by a different lens: another sufferer who sees you (Achilles and Priam), a test of whether the named cause is adequate (Spinoza), a sorting of weather from hoses (Tarr), a forum with rules (the Oresteia), forgetting (Nietzsche), an outside reader.

How to build one for a new subject:
1. Identify the process the subject is really about (not the topic). Resentment, ambition, grief, belief, self-deception, obsession, loyalty.
2. List the states in the order they unfold, in your own words, until each one plainly causes the next.
3. For each state, ask what would interrupt it, and find the lens that says so.
4. Check that each lens supplies something different. If two lenses give the same exit, keep the sharper one.

Place the chain after the lens sweep, so the audience has met each exit before it appears in the sequence.

## Choosing lenses

Match by mechanism, not topic. A lens earns its place if you can state in one sentence what it claims about the mechanism under study. If you cannot, drop it, however well it matches the subject's surface.

Selection rules:
- Five to nine lenses. Fewer than five feels thin for an enormous dive; more than nine dilutes.
- At least three periods or media (ancient, early modern, modern; text, film, television; philosophy, drama, history).
- At least one the user named, to honor their frame, and at least two they did not, to add something they could not have gotten alone.
- At least one that argues against the thesis. A dive where every lens agrees is a pep talk. The original session needed the novella in which a justified grievance becomes destruction, and the correction that rain in Tarr falls on everyone.
- For each lens, state where it breaks. Nietzsche's forgetting is a luxury of power; Spinoza's determinism may undersell anger as a signal.
- Fictional figures the user cites (a television character, a film line) count as lenses. Test them like any other and ask what the story hid.

## Crediting the user and adding your own

The user's own connections are anchors. Name them when you return to them ("your Tarr point", "your line about the rain"), and either extend them or correct them with a reason. Return to each anchor at least once in the synthesis, because that recurrence is how the piece feels like theirs.

Autonomous connections are welcome and should be marked as Claude's own: "the lens I would add" or "one more that I think belongs here." Give the mechanism-level reason for the link. Do not retrofit them to look like the user's idea.

When the user stepped back from a position, handle it as information about where the analysis sharpened, not as a win. Reflect the adjusted position accurately.

## Calibrating depth

Assume the user has done prior exploration. Skip definitions of terms on their seen-list and go straight to the use. Spend length on tensions between lenses, on facts they may not have (dates, sequences, what exactly was said), and on the weakest points of their thesis. Explain a term only when the lens is unfamiliar and the analysis depends on it.

## Common failure modes

- **Tour-guide syndrome.** A parade of references with no claim attached. Fix: every lens paragraph names its claim in its first sentence.
- **Agreement drift.** Each turn confirms the user's thesis a little more. Fix: one push per dialogue turn, and one counter-lens in the synthesis.
- **Soft close.** A last paragraph that wraps everything in uplift. Fix: end on the last substantive point.
- **Psychologizing the withheld.** Guessing at what the user has not said. Fix: write around it.
- **Spoilers by analogy.** Describing a work the user has not seen by saying how it "turns out". Fix: consult the seen-list before each reference.
- **Length by repetition.** Restating the chain in every section. Fix: state it once, late.
